# Commercial clippers read HUDs; GTA needs audio

Across eight years and several funded startups, **no commercial gameplay clipper has published an architecture, and none has ever published a precision or recall figure**. The category splits cleanly in two: event-API consumers (Medal, Overwolf/Outplayed, Nvidia Highlights) do no perception at all and simply dump a ring buffer when the game declares a kill ([Medal Auto Clipping](https://medal.tv/auto-clipping)), while pixel analysers (Eklipse, the late Powder, Sizzle, FragCut, Athenascope) fuse HUD OCR and icon matching with audio energy, laughter/shout detection, speech transcription and chat-rate spikes into an unpublished "virality score" ([Eklipse Help](https://eklipse.gg/help/how-does-eklipse-work/)). The only rigorous specification of the second family's mechanism is **NVIDIA's patent US11154773B2**, which describes percentage-coordinate HUD regions, per-region thresholding before OCR, icon detectors with confidence values, and a cue-to-event correlation script ([US11154773B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11154773B2/en)) — i.e. precisely the architecture `killclip` already implements, at an accuracy nobody in the industry has publicly matched. For GTA the honest finding is that **no vendor and no peer-reviewed paper claims semantic event detection in an open-world game**; Eklipse markets "stunts, wrecks, chases" as generic action density ([Eklipse: GTA](https://eklipse.gg/use-case/gta/)) and concedes that its manual "Clip it!" voice command "pays off hardest where there is no kill feed" ([Eklipse blog](https://blog.eklipse.gg/tools/ai-clip-maker-gaming.html)). The recommendation that follows is an audio-first cascade: an AudioSet tagger plus CLAP text prompts over the whole session as first-stage retrieval, GTA's *state* indicators (wanted stars, WASTED/BUSTED, mission text) template-matched with the machinery already in the repo, cheap motion and scene corroboration from ffmpeg, and a VLM used only to adjudicate 30–60 nominated windows — a pipeline that should run an hour of 4K60 in **under ten minutes, which is cheaper than the full 5 fps template scan already being run**.

## Nobody sells highlight detection; they sell event plumbing

The architectural fault line matters more than any feature list. Medal's documentation is explicit that "auto clipping happens in the Medal app on the player's PC, not inside your game" — the game sends "one small web request" and Medal cuts from a buffer it is already recording ([Medal Developer](https://medal.tv/developer/auto-clipping)). Nothing is detected, scored, or ranked; the game's definition of "event" *is* the entire notion of highlight. Overwolf's Game Events Provider is the same contract at a finer grain, exposing `kill`, `death`, `assist` and `kill_feed` with attacker/victim metadata for CS2, and for Marvel Rivals returning character IDs, K/D/A and ultimate charge ([Overwolf GEP: Marvel Rivals](https://dev.overwolf.com/ow-native/live-game-data-gep/supported-games/marvel-rivals)). Outplayed's automatic capture therefore simply does not exist outside integrated titles, because "the Highlights/Full Match capture mode depend on game events" ([Overwolf Support](https://support.overwolf.com/support/solutions/articles/9000284659-how-to-use-outplayed)). Nvidia Highlights never had a detector either — events are "defined by the game developer" ([Nvidia UE4 plugin](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/implementing-nvidia-highlights-plugin-unreal-engine-4)) — and the SDK now carries the notice "This is a legacy SDK… no longer supported" ([NVIDIA Highlights](https://developer.nvidia.com/highlights)). For anyone capturing console output externally, this entire family is unavailable by construction, which is the structural reason `killclip` had to exist in the first place.

The perception family is where the interesting claims live, and where the evidence is weakest. Eklipse describes "Object Detection and Optical Character Recognition (OCR)" over three signal families — visuals, audio ("shouts, laughs, and fast commentary"), and game data — with chat surges as a fourth, all rated into a "virality score" ([Eklipse: AI Highlights](https://eklipse.gg/features/ai-highlights/)). Its June 2026 "Gameplay Intelligence" rebuild moved from "cutting around a single trigger" to scoring candidate moments with per-genre logic ([Eklipse blog](https://blog.eklipse.gg/streaming-tips/how-gameplay-intelligence-works-2.html)) — architecturally a shift from detector to proposal-plus-ranker, though Eklipse never says the ranker is learned. Powder was the closest analogue to a local-first pipeline, running **over 40 proprietary game-specific models on-device** with laughter recognition, shout detection, chat-spike "Community Hype" and transcript keyword search ([TechCrunch, 20 Nov 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/20/powder-an-ai-clipping-tool-for-gaming-can-detect-when-a-creator-yells-during-a-stream/)), and claimed to invest in "self-supervised machine learning techniques for highlight detection" ([Powder blog](https://blog.powder.gg/2021/10/07/powder-building-the-camera-of-the-metaverse/)) — the single most specific ML claim any clipper has ever made, and it still names no architecture. Powder is now dead, most likely July 2026 after failing to find a sustainable business model, though every source for that date is competitor-authored SEO and should be treated as unverified ([FragCut](https://fragcut.io/alternatives/powder-gg)). Its death removes the only shipping product doing on-device multimodal detection.

Two absences in that table are worth naming. **No shipping clipper does facecam emotion detection by vision**; Powder, Eklipse and Sizzle all do emotion through *audio* — laughter, shouting, vocal energy. And **controller input as a detection signal appears only in a Microsoft patent**, never in a product. Eklipse's own cost disclosure is the clearest public datapoint on what cloud perception costs: "Processing video requires heavy GPU power," free users "share a massive pool of GPU resources" in a queue that can run "several hours" at peak, while Premium buys "dedicated high-speed GPU servers" ([Eklipse Help](https://eklipse.gg/help/why-is-my-stream-processing-stuck-or-taking-longer-than-usual/)). A business that monetises queue position is GPU-bound; a detector cheap enough for commodity CPU would not be priced this way. Against all of that, the only accuracy number that exists anywhere in the commercial space is an unattributable affiliate-review claim of **83% on Valorant** with no stated methodology, sample size, or labelling protocol — it should not be treated as an industry figure, and a locally validated 97% against in-game scoreboards is already above anything the category has disclosed.

## NVIDIA's patents are the real spec sheet, and killclip already implements it

Because the startups publish nothing, the substantive technical disclosure lives in patents held by hardware and platform companies. **NVIDIA US11154773B2** reads almost as a design document for a kill-clip detector: configuration files declare screen regions of interest (HUD, ammo, kill feed) **in percentage coordinates so they survive resolution changes**, each region gets its own preprocessing (grayscale, colour isolation, HSV, up- or down-scaling, denoise, thresholding to separate text and icons from background before OCR), detectors are OCR engines plus **icon detectors such as skull-equals-kill** plus temporal HUD-change matchers emitting confidence values, and a game-specific script correlates cues into events that drive clip extraction by timestamp ([US11154773B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11154773B2/en)). The companion **US11673061B2** adds a hierarchical event dictionary and a cue-to-event translation layer normalising heterogeneous detector output into one vocabulary ([US11673061B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11673061B2/en)). This is, line for line, what `configs/marvel-rivals.json` does with `ko_region: [0.78, 0.41, 1.0, 0.57]`, `ko_threshold: 0.75`, a set of banner-text templates, and `calib_height` scaling — convergent design arrived at independently, which is the strongest available evidence that the approach is correct rather than merely convenient.

The one published engineering trick worth stealing is in **NVIDIA US12288404B2**: HUD text is typically **10–15 px with anti-aliasing-degraded edges**, so selected regions are **upscaled 4× by bicubic interpolation *before* binarization and background removal**, because upscaling first "can provide input to the decoder that is both separated from the background and which retains visual fidelity," and region-limited upscaling bounds compute versus upscaling the full frame ([US12288404B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12288404B2/en)). The repo's `ocr_upscale: 3` is already in this territory; the patent argues for 4× and for ordering upscale strictly ahead of thresholding. The same patent names optical flow and audio pattern recognition (hit-impact sounds) as additional detectors, and is explicit that the detector can be deployed "on a player device, on the game server… or by a third party service," which is why nothing about this architecture requires the cloud.

Other patents fill in the fusion layer. **Microsoft US11850519B2** trains event recognisers on "consumable gaming content" — video frames as primary signal, audio as confirmation, controller inputs as supplementary, with an "area of interest" parameter naming the screen region ([US11850519B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11850519B2/en)). **Yahoo's US11438637B2** specifies a two-stage cascade in which a scene classifier first separates game footage from non-game footage and only game scenes reach the highlight classifier ([US11438637B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11438637B2/en)) — a cheap reliability pattern worth adopting as a loading-screen and menu reject before any scoring. **Amazon US10972524B1** is a near-complete chat-spike algorithm, computing a baseline threshold either as mean messages-per-interval or as a step function of viewer count × message count, finding runs of consecutive intervals above threshold, locating peaks inside each run, and setting boundaries by classifying the spike as anticipatory, reactive or hybrid ([US10972524B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10972524B1/en)). **Netflix US11350169B2** is the weak-supervision analogue: shot-match official trailers back to the full title to learn what a key moment looks like ([US11350169B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11350169B2/en)). A lay reading of freedom-to-operate is that a post-hoc video analyser that OCRs HUD regions *without* NVIDIA's pre-binarization upscale step reads outside its core claim, and that EA's US10346942B2 is confined to geometry-shader and draw-call interception that external capture never performs — but that is not legal advice.

## Every transcript, chat and facecam signal collapses on a silent console capture

The transcript-first tools are not merely weaker on silent gameplay; they have no boundary primitive at all. Opus Clip's documentation states plainly that **ClipBasic "relies solely on the transcript to understand spoken words,"** and the company built an entirely separate model, ClipAnything, for "videos with little to no dialogue" ([Opus Clip](https://www.opus.pro/clipanything)) — the strongest available evidence that the transcript path is not repairable rather than merely degraded. The virality score itself is a 0–99 rubric over Hook, Flow, Value and Trend with no published weights, no architecture, and no calibration against actual views ([Opus Clip help center](https://help.opus.pro/docs/article/virality-score)); it is an LLM rubric judgement rendered as a number. The most honest artefact in this whole space is an open-source clipper's production fix for speechless input: when a source has no transcribable sentences, "the transcript-based moment picker has nothing to cut," so the job **marks the file bad and fetches a different source**, giving up after five speechless files ([AI-GEMS PR #5](https://github.com/astrotobby/AI-GEMS/pull/5)). That is what graceful degradation actually looks like in this category — there isn't any.

Crowd signals are equally unavailable and, more interestingly, are *weaker than vision even when available*. The directly relevant measurement is Fu et al.'s Twitch-LoL study: on 321 League championship VODs, a chat-only character-level LSTM reaches **F ≈ 41–43** while a plain video-only ResNet-34 + LSTM over roughly 5-second windows reaches **F ≈ 68–72** on the same data, with fusion adding only about 2.5 points over vision ([EMNLP 2017](https://aclanthology.org/D17-1102.pdf)). The authors' explanation is the crux: "highlight events may have similar animations." Where the game draws an unambiguous symbol, the surrounding reaction stream is redundant. The contrast case proves the point — in commentated sports, audio-only beats vision-only (**41.79 vs 37.99 mAP**) because the useful audio is *human reaction*: "commentator reactions, audience responses, whistles, impacts," and vocal-only audio consistently outperforms background-only audio ([arXiv 2609.17923](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17923)). A silent single-player recording has none of that, and the same study finds that while highlight clips do show higher RMS and peak loudness, the distributions **overlap heavily — loudness alone is not a sufficient detector**.

Two details from the chat-spike literature are worth keeping even though chat is absent, because they generalise to any trigger-based cutting. First, every naive spike clipper is systematically late: LIGHTOR learns a backward offset of **c ≈ 20–27 seconds** from a 25-second sliding window over three min-max normalised features into a logistic regression, reaching Chat Precision@10 of 0.82 from a single training video ([LIGHTOR](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~jnwang/papers/lightor.pdf)); the open-source equivalents encode the same fact as an explicit `rollback` parameter, and Amazon's patent encodes it as spike-shape classification. Second, verified thresholds in real code are always relative to the stream's own distribution — **2 × max(p95, mean, 10) over 10-second buckets** in one Rust implementation ([finder/mod.rs](https://github.com/hougesen/twitch-highlight-finder/blob/main/highlight-finder/src/finder/mod.rs)), or a flat 15 messages per emote category per 20-second bucket in another ([config template](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/joaohenggeler/twitch-chat-highlights/main/Source/config.json.template)) — never an absolute global constant. The widely repeated "3 standard deviations over a 30-second window" formula appears **only in vendor marketing and in no open-source implementation**, and should be discarded. The convergent window sizes across every system found — 7 s, 10 s, 20 s, 25 s for scoring, with event-to-clip padding of 10 s pre / 2 s post in Google US9884258B2 and 10 s before / 5 s after the apex in the PUBG study — independently validate the repo's existing `clip_pre: 8.0` / `clip_post: 5.0` / `merge_gap: 10.0` defaults. Those numbers need no revisiting.

The one transferable idea from the crowd-signal family is **PogChampNet's**: use chat once, offline, purely as an auto-labeller — cut a 30-second clip wherever chat erupts with Pog, train a CNN on those clips, then deploy **vision-only with no chat at inference** ([PogChampNet writeup](https://medium.com/@farzatv/pogchampnet-how-we-used-twitch-chat-deep-learning-to-create-automatic-game-highlights-with-only-61ed7f7b22d4), 403-blocked, numbers unverified). The same pattern recurs as official-reel-to-raw alignment in Autohighlight and SVHighlights, as 48-dimensional colour-histogram template matching over 16 image regions in the EMNLP work, and as Netflix's trailer-matching patent. For a solo player the free label source is **their own clip history**: any session where moments were manually clipped is exactly the "this segment was clipped" supervision that line of work exploits, at zero annotation cost.

## Published numbers collapse from 94% to 29% as footage gets more like GTA

The four most load-bearing numbers in the literature, read in order, describe a cliff. AMD's **X-CLIP fine-tune reaches 94.3% overall accuracy** on FPS event detection — ViT-B/16, 32 frames at 224×224 per 1-second clip, 4,408 manually annotated events over 110 GB of CS:GO, PUBG, Valorant, Overwatch 2 and Fortnite footage, trained in 13 h 20 min on a Radeon Pro W7900, running at ~0.15 s per 1-second clip in 1.5 GB of VRAM with a 13 FPS cost when run alongside a game ([arXiv 2505.07721](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07721v1)). But its one honest generalisation test — unseen Apex Legends with generic prompts — drops **kill detection to 69%** while death detection holds at **100%**, and the paper concedes that successful death detection correlates with a "clear transition scene or display of huge and easy to detect text." The high number is a HUD-reading number. Strip the HUD and the measured result is Ringer & Nicolaou's modality ablation on 11 PUBG streams: **all views fused, 77% precision; face plus audio, 74%; audio only, 56%; face only, 52%; game footage only, 29%** ([FDG 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.09715)). Unsupervised visual novelty on raw game footage is close to useless. And at the hour scale the whole field falls over: the best method on SVHighlights — 320 sports videos averaging exactly 2.00 hours each, 640 total hours — reaches **mAP 12.81 and HIT@1 26.56**, against HIT@1 near 88 on 150-second QVHighlights clips ([SVHighlights](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06926)).

The user's own measurement that Qwen3-VL 8B, Gemma 3 27B, SigLIP 2, DINOv2 and DINOv3 fail at small HUD details is strongly corroborated and should be treated as settled. On QVHighlights validation, **Qwen2.5VL-7B zero-shot scores HIT@1 0.6 and InternVL3.5-8B scores 0.32**, while the *same* InternVL backbone with a grounding head plus query-guided token sampling reaches **88.4** ([SOTA2 leaderboard](https://www.sota2.com/research/sota/highlight-detection-on-qvhighlights-val)). Purpose-built video-LLM grounding models still underperform the older DETR line (TimeChat 23.9, VTG-LLM 33.5, versus CG-DETR 66.7). Off-the-shelf "find the exciting part" from a VLM does not work; the capability comes entirely from task-specific adaptation. Separately, CLIP and SigLIP are known-weak at reading small on-screen text, which is why a HUD-OCR index and a visual-scene embedding index are **two separate indexes solving two separate problems** and must not be conflated — a conclusion VideoSeek's design independently reaches by pairing ONNX embeddings with a distinct hard-subtitle OCR index ([VideoSeek](https://github.com/6v17/VideoSeek)). What the user's finding does *not* rule out is a VLM as a scene-level adjudicator on a handful of nominated windows, which is a different task from reading 12-pixel text and should be tested separately before being written off.

| Published figure | Transfers to silent 4K GTA capture? |
|---|---|
| AMD X-CLIP 94.3% on FPS events | **No** — event vocabulary is the FPS HUD vocabulary; its best class is a HUD transition |
| EMNLP video-only F ≈ 68–72 | **Partly** — proves supervised vision works without chat/speech, but on esports broadcast with production overlay |
| Unsupervised novelty 29% precision on game footage | **Yes, as a floor** — expect this from any zero-label saliency approach |
| RASL unsupervised 0.651 avg mAP on YouTube Highlights | **No** — 1–6 min curated sports clips; recurrence prior is far weaker when 95% of footage is urban traversal |
| SVHighlights HIT@1 26.56 at 2-hour scale | **Yes, as a ceiling** — and sports is easier (bounded event vocabulary, scoreboard, commentary) |
| LOGA: audio-visual fusion beats vision-only on all four long-form subsets ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S156625352600713X)) | **Yes** — the single most on-point result for this design decision |
| TVSum / SumMe / YouTube Highlights mAP | **No** — 25–50 videos, 1–6 min, ~10–20% highlight rate vs GTA's 1–2% |

That last row is the deepest methodological point. A two-hour GTA session has a highlight *rate* of perhaps 1–2%, which turns the problem from ranking into extreme-imbalance detection, a regime where Top-5 mAP on a three-minute video is barely well defined. The right metric for this project is **precision@k with k fixed at the number of clips actually wanted** — not mAP, and not recall, because the goal is a handful of good clips, not complete coverage. There is also **no published highlight-detection work on GTA, or on any open-world or sandbox title, at all**, and no vendor publishes a kill-feed-games-versus-open-world quality comparison. Supervised HUD detection on first-person solo console capture is unmeasured in the public literature; `killclip`'s 97% against scoreboards is, as far as this research can establish, the only validated number of its kind in existence. That cuts both ways: there is no external benchmark to beat, and no published baseline to borrow for GTA.

## GTA has no event grammar, so build one from audio onsets and state transitions

The framing that unlocks this is not "GTA has no HUD" — it has a rich one — but **GTA has no event grammar**. A kill banner is an event: the game asserts "something just happened" at a timestamp, which is why template-matching it yields 97%. GTA's HUD instead encodes *state*: wanted stars, health and armour bars, the minimap, weapon wheel, plus full-screen assertions like WASTED, BUSTED and mission pass/fail. The transferable move is therefore to **derive events from state transitions rather than looking for an event symbol** — stars going from zero to two is a chase starting, stars dropping to zero is an escape, WASTED is a death, mission text is a narrative boundary. Every one of those is a fixed percentage-coordinate region with a small grayscale template, which means a new `killclip/games/gta_v.py` reuses the existing `feed.py`/`detect.py` machinery, the `calib_height` scaling, the `ocr_upscale` path and the `merge_gap`/`clip_pre`/`clip_post` conventions unchanged. The second pillar is audio, and here GTA is unusually well served: **gunshot, machine gun, explosion, siren/emergency vehicle, screaming, vehicle/skid/crash and music are all AudioSet classes**, and GTA's sound design makes them loud and unambiguous. This is the modality the AMD paper explicitly left on the table ("we did not use the audio modality of gameplay videos"), and the one LOGA found indispensable on long-form game video.

Build it as a cascade, in this order. **Stage 0, ground truth first (half a day).** Hand-mark the good moments in two one-hour sessions, or recover them from existing clip history, and fix the metric as precision@10. Without this nothing downstream is measurable, and no external number can substitute — the complete absence of vendor precision/recall means validation must be self-built, exactly as it was against the Marvel Rivals scoreboard. **Stage 1, the audio detector (one day).** Demux to PCM and run an AudioSet tagger densely over the whole session, thresholding Gunshot, Explosion, Machine gun, Siren and Vehicle classes, then run CLAP with open-vocabulary text prompts ("police siren approaching", "car explosion", "helicopter", "tyres screeching") for concepts outside the 527-label ontology. Audio is cheap enough to run over everything and should be the **first-stage retrieval**, with visual work applied only to survivors. The repo's `audio.py` already computes log-spectrograms with a 10 ms hop and matches reference stings in a configurable band, so the plumbing — demux, resample, peak-picking with a minimum gap — exists; what is new is swapping a template for a classifier head. **Stage 2, state-transition templates (one to two days).** Add the GTA region config and detector module, plus a loading-screen/menu reject in the Yahoo cascade style. **Stage 3, fusion and budget (one day).** Score 10-second windows from audio event density, HUD state transitions, and cheap motion/scene corroboration, then nominate a **fixed count of windows per hour** rather than everything above a threshold — which makes total runtime roughly independent of recording length. **Stage 4, optional VLM adjudication.** Only after Stage 3's precision@10 is measured, and only on 20 windows as a trial before investing further.

There is deliberately no "adopt this project" row. `Aseiel/VideoHighlighter` is the closest architectural match — multi-signal scoring, explainable signal timeline, local Whisper, a macOS build — but it is **AGPL-3.0** ([repo](https://github.com/Aseiel/VideoHighlighter)), so any distributed derivative must also be AGPL, which is incompatible with the repo's MIT licence. `Segra` tracks kill/death for GTA among ten titles but is **Windows-only with no macOS build** ([repo](https://github.com/Segergren/Segra)). Every CV-based gameplay clipper on GitHub is a sub-20-star hobby repo, every turnkey text-to-video-search app is Windows-only, AGPL, or abandoned in 2022, and AMD released **no code or weights** for the one strong published gameplay result. Build from components.

On timing, the honest position is that **no trustworthy published end-to-end Mac figure exists** — the only numbers found are self-reported in a README duplicated across three accounts, spanning 90 seconds to "well over an hour" for the same hour of input depending on mode ([VideoHighlighter](https://github.com/Aseiel/VideoHighlighter)). The best anchor available is the user's own: 3 minutes per 12-minute 4K recording at 5 fps scan, i.e. **~15 minutes per hour** for the existing full template pass. Against that baseline, the cascade should come in cheaper. Audio is certainly negligible: the only on-device PANNs figure anywhere is a Pixel 8a GPU at ~0.22 s per 10-second clip (≈45× realtime) ([litert-samples PR](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/litert-samples/pull/184)), so an hour of audio is minutes at worst on an M5 Pro, and ASR — if commentary is ever added — is effectively free at **55× realtime for large-v3-turbo on M4 Max** ([vllm-mlx](https://github.com/waybarrios/vllm-mlx/blob/main/docs/benchmarks/audio.md)), with `mlx-whisper` measured **2.03× faster than whisper.cpp** on a long file and `faster-whisper` disqualified on Mac because CTranslate2 has no Metal backend ([benchmark](https://notes.billmill.org/dev_blog/2026/01/updated_my_mlx_whisper_vs._whisper.cpp_benchmark.html)). A keyframe-only tier via `-skip_frame nokey` yields roughly **1,800 frames per hour** at a typical 2-second GOP — about 1/120th of the decode work of a full 60 fps pass — and input seeking with `-ss` *before* `-i` is near-instant even on a two-hour file ([trekview](https://www.trekview.org/blog/choosing-right-frame-extraction-rate-ffmpeg/)); note that the `fps` filter saves nothing, since it decodes everything and discards most. Embedding cost is entirely a model-choice question, spanning **~6 img/s for CLIP ViT-H-14 via Core ML on M1** ([model card](https://huggingface.co/damian0815/CLIP-ViT-H-14-laion2B-s32B-b79K_CoreML)) to a directional **~1150 img/s for CLIP with ANE alongside GPU on M4 Pro** ([panopticml](https://pypi.org/project/panopticml/1.0.0/)); at ViT-B class with the ANE, 1,800 keyframes is seconds and the vision pass stops mattering. VLM adjudication is the real budget line: at ~17 tok/s generation for a 32B 4-bit model ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/notes/notes-on-early-mac-studio-ai-benchmarks-with-qwen3-235b-a22b-and-qwen2-5-vl-72b/)), 30–60 windows cost **3–7 minutes** — fine, and bounded by the window budget rather than the recording length.

Four Apple Silicon specifics will bite otherwise. The **M5 Pro has exactly one video decode engine** ([Apple Tech Specs](https://support.apple.com/en-us/126318)), so parallel ffmpeg processes contend rather than scale on the decode-bound stage; spend parallelism across stages, or on the tier-1 seek phase where per-process overhead dominates. The GPU↔CPU round-trip, not decode, is usually the bottleneck — on M4 the same 4K job ran **380 fps in HandBrake versus 145 fps in ffmpeg** because ffmpeg shuttles frames between the media engine and system memory ([HandBrake #7142](https://github.com/HandBrake/HandBrake/discussions/7142)) — so apply `scale_vt` *before* `hwdownload` and cut the bytes crossing the boundary by the square of the scale factor. On memory, a community measurement on **exactly a 48 GB M5 Pro** reports `torch.mps.recommended_max_memory()` at **37.44 GiB** while PyTorch's default high watermark ratio of 1.7 sets the ceiling at **63.6 GiB — above physical RAM**, so the machine **swaps instead of raising OOM**, which during a multi-hour job looks like "still running" with an order-of-magnitude throughput collapse; set `PYTORCH_MPS_HIGH_WATERMARK_RATIO=0.8` and `PYTORCH_MPS_LOW_WATERMARK_RATIO=0.6` together and before the first MPS call, since setting only the high ratio is a hard error ([dev.to/nefayran](https://dev.to/nefayran/mps-backend-out-of-memory-on-a-mac-what-the-numbers-mean-and-how-to-set-the-watermark-43dp)). And route the hot embedding path through Core ML rather than PyTorch MPS — the penalty is consistent at ~4× on M1 and ~3× on M4 Pro, the ANE is reachable only through Core ML and only with **static shapes**, so pad the final partial batch rather than letting a variable shape silently drop off the ANE. Apple's own M5 data predicts this workload benefits disproportionately: the Neural Accelerators gave **4× on compute-bound prefill versus 1.19–1.27× on bandwidth-bound generation** ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/exploring-llms-mlx-m5)), and batched image embedding is the archetypal compute-bound static-shape job. Two further cautions specific to this footage: the recordings are 10-bit PQ, so anything fed to CLIP, SigLIP or a VLM must be tone-mapped first or the embeddings are meaningless; and dense optical flow is impractical, because OpenCV's GPU flow implementations are CUDA-only and unavailable here — derive motion from ffmpeg's `mestimate` vectors or frame-difference energy at 320×180 instead.

Finally, what to skip. Do not fine-tune an X-CLIP-style classifier yet: there is no released code, the event vocabulary is FPS-HUD-shaped, and the 13-hour training run buys a model whose honest out-of-domain kill accuracy is 69%. Do not feed hours of video to any VLM — 2 fps over an hour is 7,200 frames, a token-budget problem before it is a memory problem. Do not expect zero-label saliency to carry the pipeline; 29% precision is the measured expectation. And do not chase mAP. Two label-free routes are worth holding in reserve once Stage 3 is measured: **weak supervision from clip history** in the Autohighlight/SVHighlights/PogChampNet style, which needs no annotation at all, and **recurrence-based novelty** scoring where "what is unusual for this session" is a defensible prior — though with the caveat that RASL's strong unsupervised result (**0.651 average mAP, beating classic supervised 0.46–0.54**, [RASL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2403.09401)) was measured on 1–6 minute curated sports clips, and the recurrence prior weakens badly when most of the footage is visually similar urban traversal. `Mr.HiSum`, at 31,892 videos labelled from YouTube "most replayed" aggregated over 50,000+ users per video ([NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/7f880e3a325b06e3601af1384a653038-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.pdf)), is the only dataset whose labels match the actual objective and the only one large enough to pretrain a general saliency head worth adapting.

## Conclusion

The sharpest realisation from this research is that the existing detector is not the wrong tool for GTA — the existing *trigger* is. `killclip` already implements the architecture that NVIDIA patented, that Eklipse markets, that `crispy` reimplements and that Opus Clip needed a whole second model to approximate: fixed percentage-coordinate regions, small templates, low frame rate, per-game confidence, pad and merge. What GTA removes is not the HUD but the game's own assertion that something happened. Rebuilding that assertion from two cheap sources — audio onsets in classes GTA renders unambiguously, and HUD *state transitions* rather than event symbols — keeps every downstream component (`cut`, EDL, `beatsync`, the merge/pad conventions, the per-game module boundary) completely unchanged, which is why this is a one-week extension rather than a new project. The realistic target is precision@10 somewhere around 60–80% with no recall guarantee, and that is a success condition, not a disappointment: at a 1–2% highlight rate over two hours, ten good clips out of twelve nominated is the whole product.

The second realisation is about calibration discipline. Every number in the published literature that looks encouraging turns out to be measuring a HUD, a commentator, a crowd, or a three-minute clip — and the one measurement taken on raw solo game footage with no labels came back at 29% precision. There is no external benchmark to target and no vendor figure to beat, because the entire commercial category competes on coverage and turnaround time while publishing nothing about quality. That makes the four local measurements — keyframe pass over a five-minute cut, 1,000 embeddings at the chosen batch size, the audio pass over five minutes, one VLM call — worth more than any figure in this report, and it makes the existing habit of validating detector changes on short cuts before full runs exactly the right instinct applied to a problem where it is now the *only* available instrument.
